01

What is AI, really?

Strip away the hype and the sci-fi.
At its core, intelligence is one thing: prediction.

The Core Idea

Intelligence is prediction
under uncertainty.

Kuthu-madhipa thappaama kanikrathu thaan intelligence.

When you catch a cricket ball, your brain isn't solving physics equations.
It's predicting where the ball will be, from thousands of past catches.

A machine that learns does exactly the same: guess, check the error, adjust. Forever.

The Map

AI is a set of nested dolls.

  • Artificial Intelligence — the big doll: any machine doing something "smart"
  • Machine Learning — inside it: machines that learn from data, not rules
  • Deep Learning — inside that: learning with many-layered neural networks
  • Generative AI — the smallest doll: deep nets that create — ChatGPT, image models
How Machines Learn — Three Ways

And you already know all three.

  • Supervised — learning with an answer key. Like studying with solved question papers.
  • Unsupervised — finding patterns with no answer key. Like sorting your wardrobe into "groups" nobody named.
  • Reinforcement — learning by reward & punishment. Like training a dog, or getting good at a video game.
02

The people they
didn't tell you about.

AI wasn't born in Silicon Valley.
It was built by outsiders, exiles, and one homeless teenager.

1943 · The First Neuron

Walter Pitts:
a homeless teenager.

At 12 he read all 2,000 pages of Russell's Principia Mathematica and mailed Russell a list of its errors. At 15 he ran away from home — for good. Homeless, sleeping in Chicago parks, he co-wrote the first mathematical model of a brain cell.

Every neural network alive today — including ChatGPT — descends from that 1943 paper. He had no degree.

1958 · The Machine That Learned

Frank Rosenblatt
& the Perceptron.

He built a room-sized machine that could learn to recognise shapes — by adjusting its own wiring. The New York Times said it was the start of computers that would "walk, talk, see, and reproduce."

He drowned in 1971 — on his 43rd birthday — before the world caught up to him.

1969 · The Winter

One book froze the
field for 15 years.

Minsky & Papert proved a single perceptron couldn't even learn XOR — a trivial "one or the other, not both" rule. Funding collapsed. This was the first AI Winter.

The fix already existed — stack the neurons in layers. The world just didn't know it yet.

1965 · The Forgotten Father

Deep learning was born
in Ukraine — in 1965.

While the West declared neural nets dead, Alexey Ivakhnenko in Soviet Ukraine was already training networks eight layers deep — the first true deep learning, decades before the word existed.

Most AI courses in English have never heard his name.

1970 · The Student Who Invented Backprop

A Finnish master's student
wrote the algorithm
that runs all of AI.

Seppo Linnainmaa, in his 1970 master's thesis, described backpropagation — the method every neural network on Earth uses to learn. He was a student. Like you.

2012 · The Big Bang

AlexNet: the night
everything changed.

A deep neural network crushed every rival at recognising images — by a margin so large the whole research world pivoted overnight. Three ingredients finally lined up: big data, GPUs, and those old 1960s ideas.

2017 · The Sentence That Made ChatGPT

"Attention is all
you need."

Eight Google researchers published the Transformer — a design that lets a model weigh which words matter to each other. Every model you've heard of — GPT, Claude, Gemini — is a Transformer.

From a homeless teenager's neuron (1943) to this (2017): one unbroken chain of ideas.

03

The mathematics.

This is the part everyone fears and no one should.
Math is not the obstacle to AI. It is the AI.

Why It Matters

You can fake a demo.
You cannot fake a foundation.

Anyone can call an AI API in ten lines. That skill expires the day the API changes.
The engineer who understands why it works can build, fix, and invent when the tools break.

Four subjects run the whole field: linear algebra, calculus, probability, information.

Foundation 1 · Linear Algebra

A vector is just
an arrow of meaning.

Meaning-a oru direction-la point panra arrow.

Every word, image, and user in an AI system becomes a list of numbers — a point in space. "King" is an arrow. "Queen" is a nearby arrow. AI measures meaning as distance and direction.

The famous magic: King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen. Meaning becomes arithmetic.

Foundation 1 · Linear Algebra

A matrix is a machine
that transforms space.

Vectors-a pudichu valaikkara oru machine.

If a vector is an arrow, a matrix is a machine that stretches, rotates, and reshapes a whole space of arrows at once. A neural network is just a tall stack of these transformations.

"Deep" learning literally means: many transformations, stacked deep.

Foundation 2 · Calculus

A derivative answers
one question:
which way is downhill?

Keezha pora vazhi edhu-nu kandupidikuradhu.

Forget the rules you memorised in school. In AI, a derivative simply tells the model, at any point, which direction reduces the error — and how steeply.

Foundation 2 · Calculus

Gradient descent:
learning blindfolded
on a mountain.

Kanna kattikitu malai-la irunthu keezha varadhu.

Imagine standing on a foggy mountain, blindfolded, trying to reach the valley. You feel the slope with your feet and step downhill. Repeat a million times. That's how every AI model learns.

Step too big, you overshoot. Too small, you never arrive. That step size is the "learning rate."

Foundation 2 · The Chain Rule

Backpropagation is just
assigning blame.

Thappu yaru mela-nu kandupidichu thiruthradhu.

When the network gets an answer wrong, backprop walks backwards through every layer asking "how much was this knob responsible for the mistake?" — then nudges each one. It's the chain rule, doing detective work.

This is Linnainmaa's 1970 idea. It trains every model you'll ever use.

Foundation 3 · Probability

Bayes: how to update
a belief with evidence.

Pudhu aadharam kedacha, pazhaya nambikkaiya maathikradhu.

A test is "99% accurate" and you test positive for a rare disease. Are you doomed? Bayes' theorem says: probably not — because the disease is rare, most positives are false alarms.

Reverend Thomas Bayes worked this out in the 1740s. It now powers spam filters, medical AI, and self-driving cars.

Foundation 3 · Probability

Markov: the future
depends only on now.

Nalai-kku nadakkurathu innaiku nadakradha vechu thaan.

Tomorrow's weather depends on today's — not on last month's. Andrey Markov turned that simple idea into chains that model language, finance, and Google's original PageRank.

Your phone's "next word" suggestion is a great-grandchild of Markov's 1900s maths.

Foundation 4 · Statistics

The bell curve is the
shape of almost everything.

Heights, exam scores, measurement errors — pile up into the same bell shape. Carl Friedrich Gauss mapped it 200 years ago. AI assumes it constantly: to model noise, uncertainty, and "normal" behaviour.

Foundation 4 · Information Theory

Shannon: information
is surprise.

Ethirpaarkadha vishayam thaan mukkiyamana information.

"The sun rose today" tells you nothing — zero surprise, zero information. "It snowed in Chennai" is huge news. Claude Shannon made surprise measurable, and called it entropy.

Every time an AI model trains, it is literally minimising surprise. That number is its loss.

The Whole Field, In One Line

Roll downhill
to reduce surprise.

Vectors hold the meaning. Calculus finds the way down. Probability handles the doubt. Information theory defines the goal.
That sentence is all of machine learning.

04

Let's open the box.

Enough theory. Let's watch a machine actually learn —
and I'll hand you the tools to do it yourself.

From Words to Numbers

Embeddings: turning
meaning into coordinates.

Vaarthaigal-a map-la points-a maathuradhu.

A model can't read "Chennai." So it assigns it a point in a 1,000-dimensional space, placed so that "Chennai," "Madras," and "Tamil Nadu" cluster together — learned purely from how humans use the words.

One Neuron, Plainly

Multiply, add, bend.

Perukki, kootti, thevaiyaana valaikkuradhu.

A neuron takes its inputs, multiplies each by a weight (how much it trusts that input), adds them up, and passes the result through a bend that decides whether to "fire."

Those weights are the knobs. Learning = finding the right weights. That's the entire game.

The Training Loop

Four steps, repeated a billion times.

  • 1 · Guess — the model predicts an answer
  • 2 · Measure — compare to the truth; compute the error (the "loss")
  • 3 · Blame — backprop finds which weights caused the error
  • 4 · Nudge — gradient descent steps every weight slightly downhill. Repeat.
How ChatGPT Actually Works

It predicts the next
word. That's the whole trick.

"The capital of France is ___." The model doesn't "know" — it computes that "Paris" is the least surprising next word. Do that one word at a time, and you get essays, code, poetry.

First it breaks text into tokens — chunks of words. "Unbelievable" might be "un + believ + able."
